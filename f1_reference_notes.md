# Formula 1: History, Evolution, Landmark Eras & Milestones

Comprehensive reference notes compiled from Wikipedia covering the history, evolution, major regulatory shifts, landmark eras, and key milestones of the FIA Formula One World Championship.

---

## 1. Focused Summary: History and Evolution of Formula 1

Formula One (F1) is the premier worldwide motorsport championship for open-wheel, single-seater racing cars. Governed and sanctioned by the Federation Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA) and commercially promoted by Formula One Group (owned by Liberty Media Corporation), F1 is widely revered as the pinnacle of global motor racing.

### Origins & Inception
- **Pre-War Roots:** Formula One evolved from the World Manufacturers' Championship (1925–1927) and European Drivers' Championship (1931–1939). Before WWII, several Grand Prix organisations proposed a new championship, but the suspension of racing during the conflict delayed formalisation.
- **Standardization (1946–1947):** In 1946, the FIA's Commission Sportive Internationale codified a new "International Formula" — designated Formula A / Formula 1 — effective in 1947. The first Grand Prix under these regulations was the 1946 Turin Grand Prix.
- **Inaugural Championship (1950):** The official FIA World Championship of Drivers launched in 1950. The first World Championship Grand Prix was held on May 13, 1950, at Silverstone, United Kingdom. Giuseppe Farina (Alfa Romeo) won the inaugural title; Juan Manuel Fangio won the next five championships (1951, 1954–1957).

### Technological and Architectural Evolution
Over its seven-decade history, F1 has transformed from an ad-hoc competition of road-car derived front-engine machines to an elite crucible of hyper-advanced aerospace engineering, cutting-edge aerodynamics, hybrid powertrains, and multi-hundred-million-dollar team operations:

1. **Front-to-Rear-Mid Engine Shift (Late 1950s):** Pioneered by Cooper and British "garagistes," relocating the engine behind the driver revolutionized chassis dynamics and road holding. By 1961, all teams had switched to mid-engined cars.
2. **Structural & Material Breakthroughs:** Transition from steel tube spaceframes to aluminum monocoque (Lotus 25, 1962), fully stressed engine integration (Lotus 49 with Cosworth DFV, 1967), and carbon-fibre composite survival cells (McLaren MP4/1, 1981).
3. **Aerodynamics & Downforce:** Introduction of wings in 1968, underbody ground-effect venturi tunnels in 1977, stepped undertrays/wooden planks in 1994, downforce trimming in 2009, and the modern venturi ground-effect revival in 2022.
4. **Powertrain Paradigm Shifts:** From 4.5L NA engines and 1.5L supercharged units in the 1950s, through the legendary Ford-Cosworth DFV V8, 1,400+ hp turbo monsters of the 1980s, screaming 3.0L V10s, high-revving 2.4L V8s, to the hyper-efficient 1.6L V6 turbo-hybrid power units with advanced ERS starting in 2014.
5. **Safety as a Defining Tenet:** Continuous safety revolutions driven by tragedy (notably the 1994 Imola weekend), resulting in crash-tested survival cells, the HANS device, trackside safety upgrades, and the titanium Halo cockpit protection system.
6. **Financial & Global Expansion:** The rise of television broadcasting, commercial sponsorships, the Concorde Agreements establishing revenue distribution, the 2021 financial Cost Cap, and expanding calendar footprints across the Americas, the Middle East, Asia, and Europe.

### Commercial Evolution
- **Bernie Ecclestone Era (1970s–2017):** Ecclestone rearranged F1's commercial rights beginning in the 1970s, transforming the series into a multi-billion dollar business. He bought Brabham in 1971, gained a FOCA seat, and became FOCA president in 1978. He offered circuits a package deal: take it or leave it, with the only requirement being trackside advertising.
- **FISA–FOCA War (1979–1981):** The formation of FISA set off a bitter conflict over television revenues and technical regulations. Resolved by the 1981 Concorde Agreement guaranteeing technical stability. Second signed 1992, third 1997.
- **Liberty Media Era (2017–Present):** Liberty Media acquired the commercial rights for an estimated $8 billion. Added five Grands Prix: Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Dutch. Introduced a new global logo in 2017, launched F1 TV streaming, and secured a new US media deal with Apple starting 2026.

## 2. Landmark Eras of Formula One

### 1. The Early Years & Front-Engine Era (1950–1957)
- **Characteristics:** Front-engined, heavy cars dominated by legacy Italian works manufacturers (Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, Maserati) and later Mercedes-Benz.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Alfa Romeo dominated 1950–1951 with the pre-war "Alfetta" 158/159. Farina won the inaugural 1950 title.
  - Fangio won five World Championships across four different marques (Alfa Romeo, Mercedes-Benz, Ferrari, Maserati) — a record standing 46 years until Schumacher's 6th in 2003.
  - 1952–1953 Championships run to Formula Two specs (2.0L NA) due to lack of competitors; Ascari and Ferrari dominated.
  - Mercedes-Benz entered in 1954 with the revolutionary W196 (desmodromic valves, streamlined bodywork), dominating until withdrawal after the 1955 Le Mans disaster.
  - Fangio's record: 24 wins out of 52 races — still the highest winning percentage by an individual driver.
  - Stirling Moss finished 2nd four times and 3rd three times between 1955–1961 without ever winning the championship.

### 2. The Rear-Mid-Engine Revolution & "Garagistes" (1958–1961)
- **Characteristics:** The engine moved behind the driver, rendering front-engine cars obsolete. British independent racing teams triumphed over traditional industrial factories.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Stirling Moss won the 1958 Argentine Grand Prix in Rob Walker's mid-engined Cooper-Climax — first World Championship win for a mid-engined car and for an independent entrant.
  - Jack Brabham won back-to-back titles in 1959 and 1960 driving mid-engined Coopers.
  - 1958: Constructors' Championship (International Cup for F1 Manufacturers) introduced; Vanwall took the first title. Mike Hawthorn became Britain's first World Champion.
  - 1961: Engine downsized to 1.5L NA; Ferrari's "sharknose" 156 won with Phil Hill; Wolfgang von Trips died at Monza.

### 3. The 1.5-Litre Era & Monocoque Innovation (1962–1967)
- **Characteristics:** Lightweight, agile cars featuring structural monocoque chassis; dominant Anglophone drivers and engineers.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Colin Chapman introduced the Lotus 25 in 1962 — first F1 car with an aluminum-sheet monocoque chassis, replacing tubular spaceframes. The greatest technological breakthrough since mid-engined cars.
  - Jim Clark dominated for Lotus, winning titles in 1963 and 1965 (also winning the Indianapolis 500 in 1965).
  - John Surtees won the 1964 championship for Ferrari — only person to win world championships on both two wheels and four wheels.
  - 1966 "Return to Power" rule (up to 3.0L NA / 1.5L forced induction). Brabham won the 1966 title in his own Repco-Brabham car — only person to win a World Championship driving a car of his own make.

### 4. The Cosworth DFV, Aerodynamics & Commercialization (1968–1976)
- **Characteristics:** Introduction of competitive customer engines, full-body commercial liveries, aerodynamic wings, and rising driver safety activism led by Jackie Stewart.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Ford-Cosworth DFV 3.0L V8 debuted in 1967 with Lotus; became the dominant customer engine for 15 years, winning 155 Grands Prix over 16 seasons — the most successful customer engine in F1 history. Democratized competitive racing.
  - 1968: First non-automotive commercial sponsorship livery — Team Gunston (Brabham) at the South African GP, then Lotus with Gold Leaf at the Spanish GP.
  - Aerodynamic downforce gained importance with aerofoils in 1968. Chapman installed modest front wings and a rear spoiler on the Lotus 49B at the 1968 Monaco GP.
  - Jackie Stewart secured titles in 1969, 1971, and 1973 while leading critical campaigns for mandatory armco barriers, medical facilities, and full-face helmets.
  - Jochen Rindt won the 1970 championship posthumously — only posthumous champion in F1 history.
  - 1976: Epic rivalry between Ferrari's Niki Lauda and McLaren's James Hunt; Lauda returned six weeks after severe burns at the Nurburgring.

### 5. The Ground-Effect Era & FISA–FOCA War (1977–1982)
- **Characteristics:** Underfloor venturi tunnels producing immense downforce; political conflict between manufacturer and privateer teams; management power struggles.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Lotus 78 (1977) and Lotus 79 (1978) introduced "ground effect" via shaped underfloor sidepods and sliding skirts. Mario Andretti became 1978 Champion. Downforce up to five times the car's weight.
  - Gordon Murray's Brabham BT46B "fan car" (1978) literally sucked air from under the floor; won its debut in Sweden, then withdrawn amid controversy.
  - Renault debuted the first 1.5L turbocharged engine (RS01) in 1977, alongside Michelin's radial tyres.
  - FISA-FOCA War: FISA (Balestre) vs FOCA (Ecclestone) over TV revenues and regulations. Resolved by 1981 Concorde Agreement guaranteeing technical stability.
  - 1981: McLaren MP4/1 — first F1 car with a carbon-fibre composite chassis. Revolutionized crash survivability.
  - FISA banned ground-effect from 1983, but turbos were already producing over 700 bhp. By 1986, BMW turbo reached estimated 1,300 bhp in qualifying.

### 6. The Turbo Era & Electronic Aids (1983–1993)
- **Characteristics:** Extraordinary horsepower, turbo boost wars, legendary rivalries, introduction of sophisticated electronic driver aids (then banned).
- **Key Highlights:**
  - 1983: Nelson Piquet (Brabham-BMW) won the first turbo-powered World Championship.
  - Turbo engines exceeded 1,300–1,400 bhp in qualifying trim (BMW M12/13, 1986).
  - 1984: Lauda beat Prost by just 0.5 points — closest championship margin in F1 history.
  - 1987: Senna won Monaco with active suspension (perfected by then).
  - 1988: McLaren-Honda with Senna and Prost won 15 of 16 races (93.75%). Senna's first title.
  - Fuel capacity limits and boost restrictions phased in: 1987 capped at 4.0 bar; 1988 at 2.5 bar + 150L fuel; turbos banned after 1988.
  - Senna's fatal crash at the 1994 San Marino GP (Tamburello curve) along with Ratzenberger's death in qualifying that same weekend marked a watershed moment.

### 7. The 3.5-Litre High-Tech & Safety Revolution Era (1994–1999)
- **Characteristics:** Drastic regulatory interventions following tragedy; rapid elevation of crash safety; heavy introduction of computerised aids (then banned); naturally aspirated 3.5L V10s/V12s/V8s.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - The catastrophic 1994 San Marino GP at Imola saw the fatal crashes of Roland Ratzenberger and three-time champion Ayrton Senna.
  - The FIA under Max Mosley immediately implemented deep safety reforms: underbody wooden skid block ("plank"), perforated airboxes, reduced displacement to 3.0L (1995), raised cockpit sides, stringent crash-test standards.
  - Michael Schumacher won his first two titles in 1994 and 1995 with Benetton.
  - Damon Hill (1996) and Jacques Villeneuve (1997) took titles with Williams.
  - 1998: Narrower cars (1.8m width) and grooved tyres (3 grooves front / 4 rear, then 4 all around) to limit cornering speeds. The "narrow track" era lasted until 2008.
  - Mika Hakkinen and McLaren-Mercedes won back-to-back championships in 1998–1999.

### 8. The V10 Golden Era & Ferrari Hegemony (2000–2005)
- **Characteristics:** Screaming 3.0L naturally aspirated V10 engines revving over 19,000 RPM; massive manufacturer investments; Ferrari domination; electronic aids banned and briefly re-introduced.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Michael Schumacher and Ferrari won five consecutive Drivers' Championships (2000–2004) and six consecutive Constructors' Championships (1999–2004).
  - 2002: Schumacher finished on the podium in every single race, clinching the title after just 11 rounds. 2004: Won 12 of the first 13 races.
  - Team orders banned in 2002 after manipulated race results, most famously the 2002 Austrian Grand Prix.
  - 2005: The US Grand Prix at Indianapolis saw seven out of ten teams refuse to race when their Michelin tyres were deemed unsafe. Bridgestone became sole supplier from 2007.
  - Fernando Alonso became F1's youngest champion at that time in 2005, ending Ferrari's dominance.

### 9. The 2.4-Litre V8 Era & Red Bull Dominance (2006–2013)
- **Characteristics:** Engine downsizing to 2.4L V8s; engine freezes and rev limits; introduction of hybrid KERS and DRS; rise of privateer teams.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Alonso won a second title in 2006. In 2007, rookie Hamilton and Alonso tied on points; Ferrari's Raikkonen snatching the title by one point.
  - Hamilton won his first title dramatically in 2008 with a final-corner overtake in Brazil — first of seven championships (equaling Schumacher's record).
  - 2008–2009: Honda, BMW, and Toyota all withdrew within a year, blaming the economic recession. Honda's team became Brawn GP via management buyout — won both world championships in its sole season.
  - 2009: Major aero reset (wide low front wings, tall narrow rear wings, return to slick tyres). Brawn GP capitalized on the double diffuser.
  - Sebastian Vettel and Red Bull, with Adrian Newey's blown-diffuser aerodynamics, dominated 2010–2013, claiming four consecutive championship doubles.
  - 2010: New teams accepted ahead of a "cost-cap": Team Lotus (Malaysian consortium), Hispania Racing (first Spanish F1 team), Virgin Racing (Richard Branson), US F1 Team. Most collapsed by 2014–2016.

### 10. The 1.6-Litre Turbo-Hybrid Era (2014–2021)
- **Characteristics:** Complex, whisper-quiet, ultra-efficient power units; Mercedes-AMG Petronas historic dominance; financial restructuring; sprint race format trials.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - 2014: 1.6L turbocharged V6 hybrid power units replacing 2.4L NA V8s. Dual MGU-K (kinetic) and MGU-H (exhaust heat). Strict 100 kg/hr fuel-flow limit.
  - Honda returned in 2015 as the championship's fourth power-unit manufacturer.
  - Mercedes mastered the split-turbo architecture, winning eight consecutive Constructors' Championships (2014–2021). Hamilton won six titles, surpassing 100 Grand Prix victories.
  - Nico Rosberg won the 2016 championship by five points, then shockingly retired.
  - 2017–2018: Revised aerodynamics produced title battles between Mercedes and Ferrari. Mercedes won both.
  - 2016: Max Verstappen became the youngest-ever race winner at age 18 in Spain.
  - 2018: Introduction of the titanium Halo cockpit protection system.
  - 2021: One of the most intense championship fights in history between Hamilton and Verstappen, culminating in Verstappen winning on the final lap in Abu Dhabi.
  - COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021): Season start delayed; races behind closed doors; 2021 car regulations pushed back to 2022; token system.
  - Sprint qualifying trialed in 2021 at three race weekends; from 2023, sprints no longer impact main race grid; separate sprint shootout introduced.

### 11. Modern Ground-Effect Era & Cost-Cap Regulation (2022–Present)
- **Characteristics:** Aerodynamic reset emphasizing 3D contoured underfloor ground-effect tunnels, 18-inch low-profile tyres, sustainable E10 fuel, financial cost parity, new entrants.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - 2022: Radical aerodynamic reboot — return of full-length contoured underfloor venturi tunnels (ground effect), simplified front wing, disappearance of bargeboards, over-wheel winglets, switch to 18-inch low-profile Pirelli tyres. Goals: reduce dirty air, promote closer racing.
  - Red Bull and Verstappen dominated: 2022 (Verstappen won 15 races); 2023 (Verstappen won 19 of 22 — 86.4% win rate; Red Bull won 21 of 22 — 95.5%, breaking McLaren's 1988 record); 2024 (Verstappen's 4th consecutive title).
  - McLaren surged back to win their first Constructors' Championship since 1998 in 2024, after sitting at the bottom of the grid in 2023.
  - August 2020: All ten F1 teams signed a new Concorde Agreement until 2025, including a $145 million budget cap.
  - 2026: Cost cap restructured to $215M operational base + $130M Power Unit Cost Cap. Audi becomes a works team (Sauber rebranded). Cadillac approved as an 11th team for 2026. Engine hybrid ratio shifting from 53/47 to 58/42 (2027) and 60/40 (2028).
  - 2025: Lando Norris (McLaren) captures his maiden World Drivers' Championship.

## 3. Key Facts: Major Rule Changes

### Powertrain & Engine Regulations
| Period | Regulation |
|---|---|
| 1947–1953 | 4.5L NA or 1.5L supercharged (1952–53 ran to 2.0L F2 specs) |
| 1954–1960 | Downsized to 2.5L NA or 750cc supercharged. Alcohol/methanol fuel banned 1958. |
| 1961–1965 | Downsized to 1.5L NA (no forced induction) |
| 1966–1985 | "Return to Power": 3.0L NA or 1.5L forced induction |
| 1986–1988 | Phased turbo restrictions: 1987 boost capped at 4.0 bar; 1988 at 2.5 bar + 150L fuel limit |
| 1989–1994 | Turbos banned; 3.5L NA engines (up to 12 cylinders) |
| 1995–2005 | Displacement reduced to 3.0L NA; V10 cylinder architecture mandatory from 2000 |
| 2006–2013 | 2.4L NA 90-degree V8. Rev limits: 19,000 RPM (2007), 18,000 RPM (2009). Engine frozen. |
| 2014–Present | 1.6L 90-degree turbo V6 hybrid. Direct injection. Max 15,000 RPM. 100 kg/hr fuel-flow. Dual ERS (MGU-K + MGU-H) |
| 2022 Fuel | Shift to E10 fuel (10% sustainable bio-ethanol blend) |
| 2026+ | Operational cost cap raised to $215M base + $130M PU cost cap. Engine-to-hybrid ratio shifting to 58/42 (2027) and 60/40 (2028) |

### Aerodynamics & Chassis Regulations
| Year | Change |
|---|---|
| 1962 | Stress-bearing monocoque chassis introduced (Lotus 25), replacing tubular spaceframes |
| 1968 | Aerodynamic wings first appeared (Lotus 49B at Monaco). Sponsorship liveries introduced |
| 1969 | Ban on high, movable wings mounted to suspension uprights; wings fixed directly to chassis |
| 1977–1982 | Ground-effect underbody tunnels with sliding skirts. Skirts banned 1981; venturi tunnels banned for 1983 |
| 1981 | McLaren MP4/1 introduces carbon-fibre composite monocoque chassis |
| 1994 | Stepped floor and central wooden/composite skid block ("plank") introduced to limit ground clearance |
| 1998 | Car width narrowed from 2,000 mm to 1,800 mm. Grooved tyres introduced |
| 2009 | Aero simplification: removal of winglets/bargeboards; wider, lower front wings; tall narrow rear wings; slick tyres reintroduced |
| 2011 | Drag Reduction System (DRS) introduced — driver-activated rear wing flap in designated zones |
| 2017 | Wider chassis (2,000 mm), wider delta-shaped wings, wider tyres (305 mm front / 405 mm rear) |
| 2018 | Mandatory titanium Halo cockpit protection |
| 2022 | Radical aero reboot: ground-effect underfloor venturi tunnels, simplified front wing, 18-inch low-profile tyres, ban on bargeboards |

### Driver Aids & Electronics
| Year | Change |
|---|---|
| 1983 | Active suspension first appeared (Lotus 92). Perfected by 1987; Senna won Monaco with it |
| 1994 | Electronic driver aids (active suspension, traction control, launch control, ABS) banned |
| 2001 | Traction control and launch control briefly re-legalized due to enforcement difficulties |
| 2004 | Launch control and full-automatic gearboxes re-banned |
| 2008 | Traction control permanently banned; standard FIA ECUs introduced |
| 2011 | Drag Reduction System (DRS) introduced for designated overtaking zones |

### Safety Milestones & Equipment
| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1968 | Dan Gurney introduces the full-face helmet to F1 |
| 1972 | Mandatory safety foam in fuel tanks; 6-point harnesses |
| 1981 | Mandatory survival cell extending to driver's feet; crash-testing introduced |
| 1994–1996 | Extensive cockpit restructuring after Imola: raised cockpit sides, headrest padding, stringent dynamic impact tests |
| 2003 | Mandatory HANS (Head and Neck Support) device |
| 2014 | FIA accident panel established after Jules Bianchi's crash; leads to Virtual Safety Car (VSC) system |
| 2018 | Mandatory titanium Halo cockpit protection — proven lifesaving (Grosjean Bahrain 2020, Zhou Silverstone 2022) |

### Sporting & Financial Rules
| Year | Change |
|---|---|
| 1958 | Constructors' Championship created |
| 1978 | Bernie Ecclestone becomes FOCA president; begins commercial restructuring |
| 1981 | First Concorde Agreement signed — codifies TV revenue sharing and governance |
| 1984 | Mid-race refuelling banned |
| 1994 | Mid-race refuelling reintroduced |
| 1992 & 1997 | Second and third Concorde Agreements signed |
| 2002 | Team orders banned after manipulated race results (2002 Austrian GP) |
| 2005 | Michelin tyre crisis at US Grand Prix; 7/10 teams refused to race |
| 2010 | Points system overhauled to modern 25-18-15-12-10-8-6-4-2-1 for top 10. Mid-race refuelling permanently banned |
| 2019 | 1 bonus point for fastest lap (drivers finishing top 10) |
| 2020 | All 10 teams sign new Concorde Agreement until 2025 with $145M budget cap |
| 2021 | Cost Cap introduced at $145M (reduced to $140M in 2022, $135M baseline thereafter) |
| 2023 | Standardized sprint weekend format established |
| 2026 | Cost cap restructured to $215M operational + $130M PU cost cap. 22-car grid |

## 6. Additional Reference Notes

### Key Figures
- **Giuseppe "Nino" Farina** — First World Champion (1950)
- **Juan Manuel Fangio** — 5 titles; record 46 years; highest winning percentage
- **Michael Schumacher** — 7 titles (equaled by Hamilton and Verstappen); 5 consecutive (2000–2004)
- **Lewis Hamilton** — 7 titles; 100+ race wins; most pole positions; most points
- **Max Verstappen** — Youngest race winner (18); 4 consecutive titles (2021–2024); record 19 wins in a season (2023)
- **Ayrton Senna** — 3 titles; legendary 1988 Monaco qualifying lap and Senna/Prost rivalry
- **Niki Lauda** — 3 titles; 0.5-point championship margin (1984); miraculous comeback after Nurburgring crash
- **Alain Prost** — 4 titles; "The Professor"
- **Jim Clark** — 2 titles; dominated 1960s; also won Indy 500
- **Jack Brabham** — 3 titles; only driver to win championship in own car
- **John Surtees** — Only person to win world championships on two and four wheels
- **Jochen Rindt** — Only posthumous World Champion (1970)

### Tyres
- **1998–2008:** Grooved tyres (3–4 grooves) to reduce cornering speeds
- **2009–Present:** Slick tyres reintroduced; exclusively supplied by Pirelli
- Three dry compounds must be used in each race; minimum one pit stop required
- 13 sets dry, 3 sets wet, 4 sets intermediate per race weekend

### Circuits
- **British and Italian Grands Prix** are the only events held every F1 season
- **Monaco Grand Prix** first held in 1929, run continuously since 1955 (except 2020)
- **Singapore 2008** — first F1 night race; followed by Abu Dhabi 2009 and Bahrain 2014
- **1950:** 7 races, 6 in Europe + Indianapolis 500 (different regulations)
- **2024:** 24 races — highest number of World Championship races in one season

### Commercial Structure
- **Concorde Agreements:** 1981 (first), 1992 (second), 1997 (third), 2020 (fourth — until 2025)
- **Entry fee:** $450 million up-front payment for new teams (not for 2026; restructured framework)
- **Driver Super Licence:** Highest FIA racing licence required
- **Commercial rights:** Liberty Media (acquired 2017 for ~$8 billion); previously Bernie Ecclestone

### Physical Demands
- Drivers burn ~1,000 calories per hour; lose up to 5% body weight per race
- Cornering forces up to 6.5g; braking 6g; acceleration 2g
- Cockpit temperatures up to 60°C (140°F)
- Minimum driver weight: 82 kg (181 lb)
- Heart rates average >170 bpm during races

### Points System (Current — since 2010)
- Top 10 finishers score: 25-18-15-12-10-8-6-4-2-1
- Driver must complete 90% of race distance to receive points
- 1 bonus point for fastest lap (if driver finishes in top 10)
- Since 2022: Gradual-scale half-points rule based on race distance completed

### Sprint Format (Since 2023)
- Friday: One free practice + Sprint Shootout (determines sprint grid)
- Saturday: 100 km Sprint race (top 8 score points) + traditional Qualifying (determines main race grid)
- Sunday: Main Grand Prix
