## History & Evolution — Summary

Formula One (F1) is the highest class of worldwide racing for open-wheel, single-seater formula racing cars, run by the Formula One Group and sanctioned by the **Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA)**. The FIA Formula One World Championship has been one of the world's premier forms of motorsport since its **inaugural running in 1950** and is widely considered to be the pinnacle of motorsport.

The word *formula* in the name refers to the set of rules all participant cars must follow. A Formula One season consists of a series of races known as **Grands Prix**, which take place in multiple countries and continents on either purpose-built circuits or closed public roads (street circuits).

A **points scoring system** is used at each Grand Prix to determine two annual World Championships:
- **Drivers' World Championship**
- **Constructors' World Championship** (synonymous with teams)

Each driver must hold a valid **Super Licence** — the highest class of racing licence issued by the FIA — and all races must be held on **Grade One tracks**, the FIA's highest circuit rating.

Formula One cars are the **world's fastest regulated road-course racing cars**, achieving extraordinary cornering speeds through massive aerodynamic downforce generated by front and rear wings and underbody tunnels. The cars are complex machines dependent on electronics, aerodynamics, suspension, and tyres.

### Origins & Founding

Formula One originated from two predecessor championships:
- **World Manufacturers' Championship (1925–1927)**
- **European Drivers' Championship (1931–1939)**

Before World War II, several Grand Prix racing organisations proposed a new championship to replace the European Championship, but the suspension of racing during the conflict delayed formalisation. The FIA formally established the Formula One regulations in **1946**, effective from **1947**. The first Grand Prix under the new regulations was the **1946 Turin Grand Prix**. The inaugural FIA Formula One World Championship season commenced in **1950**.

### The First Championship Race

The first world championship race, the **1950 British Grand Prix**, took place at **Silverstone Circuit** in the United Kingdom on **13 May 1950**. **Giuseppe Farina**, competing for Alfa Romeo, won the first Drivers' World Championship, narrowly defeating his teammate **Juan Manuel Fangio**. Fangio went on to win the championship in **1951, 1954, 1955, 1956, and 1957** — a record for the most World Championships won by a single driver that stood for **46 years** until Michael Schumacher won his sixth title in 2003.

Fangio won **24 of the 52 races** he entered — still the record for the highest F1 winning percentage by an individual driver.

### The Constructors' Championship & Early Milestones

- **1958**: The Constructors' Championship was added to the World Championship.
- **1962**: Lotus introduced a car with an **aluminium-sheet monocoque chassis** instead of the traditional space-frame design — the greatest technological breakthrough since the mid-engine revolution.
- **1968**: **Sponsorship was introduced** to the sport. Team Gunston became the first team to run cigarette sponsorship; Lotus became the first works team to follow with Gold Leaf livery at the Spanish Grand Prix. Aerodynamic **aerofoils (wings)** also appeared this season, introduced by Colin Chapman at Monaco.
- **1983**: The last non-championship Formula One race — the Race of Champions at Brands Hatch, won by Keke Rosberg.

### Commercial Transformation

Beginning in the **1970s**, **Bernie Ecclestone** rearranged the management of F1's commercial rights, transforming the series into a multi-billion dollar business. He bought the Brabham team in 1971, gained a FOCA seat, and became FOCA president in 1978. He persuaded teams to "hunt as a pack" and offered circuits a package deal.

The **FIA–FOCA war** (1979–1981) set off a power struggle over television revenues and technical regulations, resulting in the **1981 Concorde Agreement** guaranteeing technical stability. Additional Concorde Agreements followed in **1992** and **1997**.

The commercial rights were later acquired by **Liberty Media Corporation in 2017** for an estimated **$8 billion**, previously held by Bernie Ecclestone. Under Liberty Media, five new Grands Prix were added: **Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Dutch**.

## Major Rule Changes & Regulatory Eras

### 1994 — The Ban on Electronic Aids
- Traction control, launch control, and automatic shifting were **first banned**.
- Marked a shift toward emphasizing **driver skill** over technological intervention.
- Made the cars more challenging and unpredictable to drive.
- Occurred in the same season as the deaths of Ayrton Senna and Roland Ratzenberger at the San Marino Grand Prix.

### 1998–2008 — The Narrow-Track & Grooved Tyre Era
- FIA imposed a ban on ground-effect aerodynamics from 1983, but turbos were still producing 1,100+ bhp by 1987.
- To reduce speeds: **fuel tank capacity limited (1984)**, **boost pressures limited (1988)**, **turbos banned (1989)**.
- After Senna's death, the FIA used safety as justification for the **'narrow track' era (1998 onwards)**: smaller rear tyres, narrower track, grooved tyres to reduce mechanical grip.
- Objective: reduce cornering speeds to produce racing similar to rainy conditions.

### 2001–2008 — The Oscillating Electronic Aids Ban
- Electronic aids were **briefly reintroduced in 2001**.
- Banned again in **2004**, then **fully banned in 2008**.
- Reflected the ongoing tension between F1's technological innovation mandate and its roots in raw driving talent.

### 2009 — Slick Tyres Return & Aerodynamic Reset
- **Slick tyres reintroduced** after 11 years of grooved tyres.
- Major aerodynamic changes to improve racing.
- Bridgestone became sole tyre supplier for 2007–2009.

### 2014 — The Hybrid Revolution
- **2.4-litre naturally aspirated V8 engines replaced by 1.6-litre turbocharged hybrid power units**.
- Honda returned to the series as the championship's fourth power-unit manufacturer.
- Energy recovery technology (MGU-K, MGU-H) became central to power unit design.
- Mercedes emerged as the dominant force.

### 2022 — Ground-Effect Cars & Aerodynamic Reset
- A major rule and car design change to **promote closer racing**.
- **Ground effects returned** alongside redesigned front and rear wings.
- **Larger wheels with low-profile tyres** (18-inch).
- **Barge boards banned**; redesigned nose and wing regulations.
- Designed to reduce turbulent air ("dirty air") and enable tighter following.

### Cost Cap Era (2021–present)
- Introduced to **level the playing field** between wealthy and smaller teams.
- **2021**: $145M budget cap introduced (reduced from $175M due to COVID-19).
- **2022**: Reduced to $140M.
- **2023–2025**: Settled at $135M.
- **2026**: Fundamentally restructured — **operational cap increased to $215M base** (absorbing previously excluded expenditures), plus a separate **$130M Power Unit Cost Cap**.
- **Exclusions** (e.g., certain senior salaries, marketing costs) mean actual spending often exceeds the cap.
- Has fuelled significant **financial and political battles** within the paddock.

## Landmark Rivalries & Tragedies

### Senna vs. Prost (1988–1993)
- The rivalry between **Ayrton Senna** and **Alain Prost** became F1's central focus in 1988 and continued until Prost retired at the end of 1993.
- Their on-track battles defined an era.

### The 1994 San Marino Grand Prix — A Turning Point
- **Ayrton Senna** died at the Tamburello curve after crashing into a wall.
- **Roland Ratzenberger** also died in an accident during Saturday qualifying.
- These tragedies prompted the FIA to use safety as justification for rule changes that would otherwise require team agreement under the Concorde Agreement.
- Resulted in the narrow-track era, grooved tyres, and reduced cornering speeds.

### Bianchi's Fatal Crash (2014–2015)
- **Jules Bianchi** collided with a recovery vehicle after aquaplaning at the 2014 Japanese Grand Prix.
- He died on **17 July 2015** from his injuries — the first driver death from on-track injuries in 20 years.
- Led to the introduction of the **Virtual Safety Car (VSC)** system, first implemented at the 2015 Monaco Grand Prix.

## The Concorde Agreements

The Concorde Agreements are the pivotal commercial contracts that have shaped F1's financial and regulatory landscape:

1. **1981**: First Concorde Agreement — guaranteed technical stability, teams given reasonable notice of new regulations. Ended the FISA–FOCA war.
2. **1992**: Second Concorde Agreement.
3. **1997**: Third Concorde Agreement.
4. **2020**: All 10 F1 teams signed a new Concorde Agreement committing them to the sport until **2025**, including a **$145 million budget cap** for car development.

## Structural & Regulatory Themes

- **Dual championship structure**: Drivers' + Constructors' championships create a dual competitive focus.
- **Super Licence requirements**: Serve as a barrier to entry, ensuring only top-tier drivers compete (requires 300 km of running in an F1 car within 2 days).
- **Grade One circuit standards**: Ensure the highest safety and facility levels at all F1 venues.
- **Evolving cost cap framework**: Continues to shape team budgets and financial politics within the sport.
- **Concorde Agreement cycle**: Commercial agreements periodically reset the sport's financial and regulatory framework.

## Geographic & Commercial Expansion

### UK Engineering Hub
- The **United Kingdom** hosts **6 out of 11** F1 teams.
- Centred around Oxfordshire — known as **"Motorsport Valley."**
- Long regarded as the technical and engineering heart of the sport.
- **Ferrari** is the only team without an English presence; chassis and engine assembly are in Maranello, Italy.

### Calendar Expansion Under Liberty Media
- Five new Grands Prix added since 2017: **Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Netherlands**.
- Reflects a strategic shift toward **global market growth**, particularly in the U.S. and Middle East.
- Growth of **street circuits** (Las Vegas, Miami, Jeddah, Lusail) as spectacle-driven events.
- The **Las Vegas Grand Prix** entered the series in 2023.

### Broadcast Rights Evolution
- **2001**: Cumulative TV audience of 54 billion across 200 territories (since declined to ~1.55 billion).
- **2012**: Sky Sports F1 launched in the UK.
- **2013**: ESPN acquired U.S. rights (~$90M/year as of 2025).
- **2017**: F1 TV streaming platform launched.
- **2025**: Apple Inc. acquired U.S. rights under a 5-year deal; most coverage exclusive to Apple TV.
