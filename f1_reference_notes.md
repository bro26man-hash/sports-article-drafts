# Formula One — Reference Notes

## Overview

Formula One (F1) is the highest class of worldwide racing for open-wheel, single-seater formula racing cars, run by the Formula One Group and sanctioned by the Federation Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA). The FIA Formula One World Championship has been one of the world's premier forms of motorsport since its inaugural running in **1950** and is often considered the pinnacle of motorsport.

The word *formula* in the name refers to the set of rules all participant cars must follow. A Formula One season consists of a series of races known as Grands Prix, held in multiple countries and continents on either purpose-built circuits or closed road circuits. A points-scoring system determines two annual World Championships: one for drivers and one for constructors (teams).

## History & Evolution

### Origins (1925–1949)

Formula One originated from two predecessor championships:
- **World Manufacturers' Championship (1925–1927)**
- **European Drivers' Championship (1931–1939)**

Before World War II, several Grand Prix racing organisations had suggested creating a new championship to unify the sport under a single set of regulations. The FIA formally established the Formula One World Championship in 1946, with the first season taking place in **1950**. The first Grand Prix under the new regulations was the **1946 Turin Grand Prix**.

### The 1950s — Founding Era

- The inaugural F1 World Championship race was the **1950 British Grand Prix** at Silverstone on **13 May 1950**.
- **Giuseppe Farina** (Alfa Romeo) won the first Drivers' World Championship, narrowly defeating teammate **Juan Manuel Fangio**.
- **Juan Manuel Fangio** won five World Championships (1951, 1954, 1955, 1956, 1957) — a record of 5 titles that stood for 46 years until Michael Schumacher won his sixth in 2003. Fangio won **24 of 52 races** — still the highest winning percentage by an individual driver.
- **Stirling Moss** is regarded as one of the greatest drivers never to win the F1 championship (2nd four times, 3rd three times between 1955–1961).
- Early seasons were dominated by Italian manufacturers (Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, Maserati, Lancia).
- A **Constructors' Championship** was added in **1958**.
- Non-championship F1 races continued until **1983**.

### The 1960s — Aerodynamic Revolution

- **Mid-engined cars**: Bugatti introduced them first; Jack Brabham proved their superiority. By 1961, all teams had switched. The Ferguson P99 (four-wheel drive) was the last front-engined F1 car in a championship race (1961 British GP).
- **Monocoque chassis**: Lotus introduced an aluminium-sheet monocoque in 1962 — the greatest technological breakthrough since mid-engined cars.
- **Sponsorship**: First introduced in 1968; Team Gunston ran cigarette sponsorship (Gold Leaf livery on Lotus at the 1968 Spanish GP).
- **Aerodynamic downforce**: Aerofoils appeared in 1968; Lotus's Colin Chapman installed modest wings at the 1968 Monaco GP. In the late 1970s, Lotus introduced **ground-effect aerodynamics** (previously used on Jim Hall's Chaparral 2J in 1970) that generated enormous downforce — up to 5x the car's weight. This required extremely stiff springs, leaving suspension virtually solid.
- **Tyrrell P34**: The six-wheeled car (1976–78), the most radical design ever raced.

### Big Business and the Concorde Era (1970s–1990s)

- **Bernie Ecclestone** rearranged F1's commercial rights from the 1970s, transforming it into a multi-billion-dollar business. When he bought Brabham in 1971, he gained a FOCA seat; became FOCA president in 1978. He offered circuits a package deal — surrender trackside advertising in exchange for the whole package.
- The **FISA-FOCA war** (1979 onward): FISA (president Jean-Marie Balestre) vs. FOCA over TV revenues and technical regulations.
- The **1981 Concorde Agreement** guaranteed technical stability — teams would get reasonable notice of new regulations. Second in 1992, third in 1997.
- **Turbocharged engines**: Renault pioneered them in 1977; by 1986, BMW turbo engines produced over 1,300 bhp in qualifying (5.5 bar / 80 psi). By 1987, race trim power reached ~1,100 bhp. The FIA limited fuel tank capacity (1984), boost pressures (1988), then **banned turbos entirely in 1989**.
- **Electronic driver aids**: Active suspension (Lotus 92, 1983; perfected by 1987 — Senna won Monaco in it). Semi-automatic gearboxes and traction control followed. The FIA **banned most aids for 1994**, making cars very "twitchy and difficult to drive."

### The Senna Era and Safety Reforms (1988–1994)

- The **Senna vs. Prost rivalry** became F1's central focus in 1988 and continued until Prost retired at the end of 1993.
- **Ayrton Senna** died at the **1994 San Marino Grand Prix** after crashing into a wall on the exit of the Tamburello curve. **Roland Ratzenberger** also died in an accident during Saturday qualifying that weekend.
- After these tragedies, the FIA used safety as justification to impose rule changes unilaterally (bypassing the Concorde Agreement's requirement for all-team agreement).
- The **"narrow track" era** (1998 onward): smaller rear tyres, narrower track overall, and **grooved tyres** (four circumferential grooves) to reduce mechanical grip and cornering speeds.
- No driver died of injuries sustained on track for 20 years until **Jules Bianchi**'s fatal crash at the 2014 Japanese Grand Prix (died 17 July 2015).

### The Schumacher Dynasty (1990s–2006)

- **Michael Schumacher** and **Ferrari** won **five consecutive Drivers' Championships** (2000–2004) and **six consecutive Constructors' Championships** (1999–2004).
- Schumacher set many new records: Grand Prix wins, wins in a season, and most Drivers' Championships (7, tied with Hamilton).
- Schumacher's championship streak ended on **25 September 2005** when Renault driver **Fernando Alonso** became champion.
- Schumacher retired at the end of 2006 after 16 years, but came out of retirement for 2010, racing for the newly formed Mercedes works team.
- **Team orders** were legal since 1950 but were **banned in 2002** after several incidents of teams openly manipulating race results, most famously by Ferrari at the **2002 Austrian Grand Prix**.

### The Hybrid Era (2014–Present)

- **2014**: 2.4-litre naturally aspirated V8 engines replaced by **1.6-litre turbocharged hybrid power units**. This prompted Honda to return to the series in 2015.
- **Mercedes** emerged as the dominant force: Lewis Hamilton won the championship, closely followed by teammate Nico Rosberg, with the team winning 16 out of 19 races in 2014. 16 wins in 2015; record 19 wins in 2016. Hamilton claimed the title in 2014, 2015, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020; Rosberg won in 2016 by five points then retired.
- **Max Verstappen** became the youngest-ever race winner at age 18 in Spain (2016).
- **2017–2018**: Revised aerodynamic regulations; title battle between Mercedes and Ferrari; Mercedes won with multiple races to spare.
- **2021**: Honda-powered Red Bull began to seriously challenge Mercedes; **Verstappen beat Hamilton to the Drivers' Championship** after a season-long battle.
- **2020**: COVID-19 pandemic forced adaptation — the 2021 technical regulations were pushed back to 2022, with constructors using 2020 chassis for two seasons and a token system limiting which parts could be modified.
- **2022**: Major rule and car design change — **ground effects reintroduced**, new aerodynamics, larger wheels with low-profile tyres, redesigned nose and wing regulations. **Red Bull emerged as the dominant force** after the rule shakeup.
- **2024**: Sauber introduced title partnership with Stake, becoming **Stake F1 Team Kick Sauber**. Will become the **Audi works team for 2026**. AlphaTauri rebranded as **Visa Cash App RB (VCARB)**.
- **2025**: **McLaren** won the Constructors' Championship; Hamilton joined Ferrari.
- **2025–2026**: **Cadillac** received final approval to join the F1 grid as an 11th team for the 2026 season onward.

### The 2020s — New Regulations & Cost Cap

- **Cost cap introduced**: As of the 2026 season, the operational cost cap stands at **US$215 million**, with a separate **$130M Power Unit cost cap**.
- **New Power Unit regulations for 2026**: removal of the MGU-H, greater reliance on fully sustainable fuels. Engine-to-hybrid power split shifting from 53/47 → 58/42 (2027) → 60/40 (2028), increasing max ICE output to 450kW.
- **Customer cars**: At least two teams must sell cars to smaller teams.
- **2026 grid**: 11 teams with **Cadillac** joining; **Audi** takes over Sauber; **VCARB** (ex-AlphaTauri) rebranded.

## The Regulatory Cycle — A Thematic Lens

F1 history follows a recurring pattern: **innovation → dominance → regulation → reset**. Each era's defining technology was eventually regulated to maintain competition and emphasize driver skill over engineering budget:

1. **Mid-engine dominance** (1961) → standardised layout, killed front-engine design
2. **Ground effect** (late 1970s) → banned in 1982/83 due to fatal accidents and extreme speeds
3. **Turbo era** (1977–1989) → progressively restricted, then banned outright
4. **Electronic driver aids** (early 1990s) → banned 1994, briefly returned 2001, banned again 2004/2008
5. **Team orders** → legal since 1950, banned 2002 after Ferrari's deliberate crash at the Austrian GP
6. **Hybrid complexity** (2014+) → cost cap introduced to prevent engineering budget from determining outcomes

This cycle is a central narrative thread for any F1 background piece — the sport constantly grapples with the tension between technological progress and competitive parity.

## Commercial Landscape

- **Liberty Media** acquired commercial rights in **2017 for approximately $8 billion** (previously owned by Bernie Ecclestone).
- Added 5 new Grands Prix to the calendar: Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Dutch.
- **Cost cap progression**:
  - 2021: $175M (initial cap)
  - 2022: $145M (COVID-19 adjustment)
  - 2023: $140M
  - 2024–2025: $135M
  - 2026+: **$215M** (restructured to absorb previously excluded expenditures)
- **Power Unit Cost Cap**: $130M (separate, parallel cap introduced 2026).
- **Team entry fee**: $500,000 plus $5,000 per point scored the previous year; new teams pay $450M up-front.
- **Super Licence fee**: €10,000 + €1,000 per point.
- Criticism: The cost cap excludes driver salaries, compensation for the three highest-paid staff members, and marketing costs.
- Notable success story: McLaren went from slowest car mid-2023 to fastest on the grid by 2024, winning the Constructors' Championship.

## Future Outlook

- **Environmental impact**: F1 targets carbon neutrality by **2030**; E10 fuel (10% ethanol) from 2021; **100% sustainable fuel** planned for 2025/2026.
- **Engine regulations 2026+**: New power-unit cycle with hybrid split shifting from 53/47 → 58/42 (2027) → 60/40 (2028), increasing max ICE output to 450kW.
- **2026 grid**: 11 teams with **Cadillac** joining; **Audi** takes over Sauber; **VCARB** (ex-AlphaTauri) rebranded.
- **Concorde Agreement**: All 10 teams signed through **2025**; new agreement needed for 2026+.
- **Apple TV+** acquired US rights from ESPN ahead of the **2025 United States Grand Prix** — a landmark shift in US broadcasting.
