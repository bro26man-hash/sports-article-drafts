# Formula 1: History, Evolution, Landmark Eras & Milestones

Comprehensive reference notes compiled from Wikipedia covering the history, evolution, major regulatory shifts, landmark eras, and key milestones of the FIA Formula One World Championship.

---

## 1. Focused Summary: History and Evolution of Formula 1

Formula One (F1) is the premier worldwide motorsport championship for open-wheel, single-seater racing cars. Governed and sanctioned by the Federation Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA) and commercially promoted by Formula One Group (owned by Liberty Media Corporation), F1 is widely revered as the pinnacle of global motor racing.

### Origins & Inception
- **Pre-War Roots:** Formula One evolved from the Grand Prix motor racing of the 1920s and 1930s held across Europe.
- **Standardization (1946-1947):** In 1946, the Commission Sportive Internationale (CSI) of the FIA codified a new set of rules-the "International Formula"-to create a level playing field, designating it Formula A / Formula 1, taking effect in 1947. The "formula" refers to the strict set of technical and sporting regulations all competitors must follow.
- **Inaugural Championship (1950):** The official FIA World Championship of Drivers was launched in 1950, with the first World Championship Grand Prix held on May 13, 1950, at Silverstone, United Kingdom.

### Technological and Architectural Evolution
Over its seven-decade history, Formula One has transformed from an ad-hoc competition of road-car derived front-engine machines to an elite crucible of hyper-advanced aerospace engineering, cutting-edge aerodynamics, hybrid powertrains, and multi-hundred-million-dollar team operations:
1. **Front-to-Rear-Mid Engine Shift (Late 1950s):** Pioneered by Cooper and British "garagistes," relocating the engine behind the driver revolutionized chassis dynamics and road holding.
2. **Structural & Material Breakthroughs:** Transition from steel tube spaceframes to aluminum monocoque (Lotus 25, 1962), fully stressed engine integration (Lotus 49 with Cosworth DFV, 1967), and carbon-fibre composite survival cells (McLaren MP4/1, 1981).
3. **Aerodynamics & Downforce:** Introduction of wings in 1968, underbody ground-effect venturi tunnels in 1977, stepped undertrays/wooden planks in 1994, downforce trimming in 2009, and the modern venturi ground-effect revival in 2022.
4. **Powertrain Paradigm Shifts:** Ranging from giant 4.5L naturally aspirated engines and 1.5L supercharged units in the 1950s, through the legendary Ford-Cosworth DFV V8, 1,400+ hp turbo monsters of the 1980s, screaming 3.0L V10s, high-revving 2.4L V8s, to the hyper-efficient 1.6L V6 turbo-hybrid power units with advanced Energy Recovery Systems (ERS) starting in 2014.
5. **Safety as a Defining Tenet:** Continuous safety revolutions driven by tragedy (notably the 1994 Imola weekend), resulting in crash-tested survival cells, the HANS device, trackside safety upgrades, and the titanium Halo cockpit protection system.
6. **Financial & Global Expansion:** The rise of television broadcasting, commercial sponsorships, the Concorde Agreements establishing revenue distribution, the 2021 financial Cost Cap, and expanding calendar footprints across the Americas, the Middle East, Asia, and Europe.

### Commercial Evolution
- **Bernie Ecclestone Era (1970s-2017):** Ecclestone rearranged the management of F1's commercial rights beginning in the 1970s, transforming the series into a multi-billion dollar business. He bought Brabham in 1971, gained a FOCA seat, and became FOCA president in 1978.
- **Liberty Media Era (2017-Present):** Liberty Media acquired the commercial rights in 2017 for an estimated $8 billion. Liberty Media added five Grands Prix to the calendar: Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Dutch.

## 2. Landmark Eras of Formula One

### 1. The Early Years & Front-Engine Era (1950-1957)
- **Characteristics:** Front-engined, heavy cars dominated by legacy Italian works manufacturers (Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, Maserati) and later Mercedes-Benz.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Alfa Romeo dominated 1950-1951 with the pre-war "Alfetta" 158/159. Giuseppe "Nino" Farina won the inaugural 1950 title.
  - Juan Manuel Fangio emerged as the master of the era, winning five World Championships (1951, 1954, 1955, 1956, 1957) across four different marques (Alfa Romeo, Mercedes-Benz, Ferrari, Maserati).
  - Due to Alfa Romeo's withdrawal and lack of competitors, the 1952-1953 World Championships were run to Formula Two specifications (2.0L naturally aspirated), dominated by Alberto Ascari and Ferrari.
  - Mercedes-Benz entered in 1954 with the revolutionary W196 (featuring desmodromic valves and streamlined bodywork), dominating until their withdrawal following the 1955 Le Mans disaster.
  - Fangio's record: 24 wins out of 52 races entered-still the record for the highest winning percentage by an individual driver.
  - Stirling Moss, often regarded as one of the greatest drivers of the 1950s and 1960s, never won the F1 championship, finishing 2nd four times and 3rd three times between 1955 and 1961.

### 2. The Rear-Mid-Engine Revolution & "Garagistes" (1958-1961)
- **Characteristics:** The engine moved behind the driver, rendering front-engine cars obsolete. British independent racing teams ("garagistes") triumphed over traditional industrial factories.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - In 1958, Stirling Moss won the Argentine Grand Prix in Rob Walker's mid-engined Cooper-Climax, the first World Championship win for a mid-engined car and for an independent entrant.
  - Jack Brabham won back-to-back titles in 1959 and 1960 driving mid-engined Coopers.
  - 1958 marked the introduction of the International Cup for F1 Manufacturers (Constructors' Championship), won by Vanwall, and Mike Hawthorn became Britain's first World Champion.
  - 1961 saw engine displacement downsized to 1.5L naturally aspirated; Ferrari's "sharknose" 156 won the title with Phil Hill, shadowed by the fatal accident of Wolfgang von Trips at Monza.

### 3. The 1.5-Litre Era & Monocoque Innovation (1962-1967)
- **Characteristics:** Lightweight, agile cars featuring structural monocoque chassis; dominant Anglophone drivers and engineers.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Colin Chapman introduced the Lotus 25 in 1962, the first F1 car with an aluminum sheet monocoque chassis, replacing tubular spaceframes-the greatest technological breakthrough since the introduction of mid-engined cars.
  - Jim Clark dominated the era for Lotus, winning titles in 1963 and 1965 (also winning the Indianapolis 500 in 1965).
  - John Surtees won the 1964 championship for Ferrari, becoming the only person in motorsport history to win world championships on both two wheels and four wheels.
  - 1966 brought the "Return to Power" rule change (up to 3.0L naturally aspirated / 1.5L forced induction). Jack Brabham won the 1966 championship in his own Repco-Brabham car, remaining the only person to win a World Championship driving a car of his own make.

### 4. The Cosworth DFV, Aerodynamics & Commercialization (1968-1976)
- **Characteristics:** Introduction of off-the-shelf competitive customer engines, full-body commercial liveries, aerodynamic wings, and rising driver safety activism led by Jackie Stewart.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - The Ford-Cosworth DFV 3.0L V8 debuted in 1967 with Lotus and became the dominant customer engine for the next 15 years, winning 155 Grands Prix over 16 seasons-the most successful customer engine in F1 history. It democratized competitive racing.
  - 1968 introduced non-automotive commercial sponsorship livery: Team Gunston was the first to run cigarette sponsorship on its Brabham cars at the 1968 South African Grand Prix. Lotus followed at the 1968 Spanish Grand Prix with Gold Leaf red, white, and gold.
  - Aerodynamic downforce slowly gained importance with the appearance of aerofoils during 1968. Colin Chapman installed modest front wings and a rear spoiler on his Lotus 49B at the 1968 Monaco Grand Prix.
  - Jackie Stewart secured titles in 1969, 1971, and 1973, while leading critical campaigns for mandatory armco barriers, medical facilities, and full-face helmets (first used in 1968 by Dan Gurney).
  - Jochen Rindt won the 1970 championship posthumously for Lotus following his fatal crash at Monza-the only posthumous champion in F1 history.
  - The epic 1976 rivalry between Ferrari's Niki Lauda and McLaren's James Hunt; Lauda miraculously returned just six weeks after severe burns at the Nurburgring.

### 5. The Ground-Effect Era & FISA-FOCA War (1977-1982)
- **Characteristics:** Utilization of underfloor venturi tunnels to produce immense downforce; political conflict between manufacturer teams and privateer teams; management/business power struggles.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Colin Chapman's Lotus 78 (1977) and Lotus 79 (1978) introduced "ground effect" via shaped underfloor sidepods and sliding skirts, drastically increasing cornering speeds. Mario Andretti became the 1978 World Champion. The aerodynamic forces pressing cars to the track were up to five times the car's weight.
  - Gordon Murray designed the Brabham BT46B "fan car" in 1978, which literally sucked air from under the floor; it won its debut in Sweden and was immediately withdrawn amid controversy.
  - Renault debuted the first 1.5L turbocharged engine (RS01) in 1977, alongside Michelin's radial tyres.
  - FISA-FOCA War: The formation of FISA in 1979 set off a bitter conflict between FISA (president Jean-Marie Balestre) and FOCA (president Bernie Ecclestone) over television revenues and technical regulations. Resolved by the 1981 Concorde Agreement, which guaranteed technical stability.
  - In 1981, John Barnard and McLaren introduced the MP4/1, the first F1 car built with a carbon-fibre composite chassis-revolutionizing crash survivability and chassis stiffness.
  - FISA imposed a ban on ground-effect aerodynamics from 1983, but by then turbocharged engines were producing over 700 bhp. By 1986, a BMW turbocharged engine achieved a flash reading of 5.5 bar, estimated at over 1,300 bhp in qualifying.

### 6. The Turbo Era & Electronic Aids (1983-1993)
- **Characteristics:** Extraordinary horsepower levels, intense turbo boost wars, legendary driver rivalries, and the introduction of sophisticated electronic driver aids.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - In 1983, Nelson Piquet in a Brabham-BMW won the first World Championship powered by a turbocharged engine.
  - Turbo engines reached absurd outputs in qualifying trim-exceeding 1,300 to 1,400 bhp (BMW M12/13 1.5L 4-cylinder engine in 1986).
  - In 1984, Niki Lauda edged McLaren teammate Alain Prost by just 0.5 points-the closest championship margin in F1 history.
  - In 1987, Ayrton Senna won Monaco in a Williams with active suspension-perfected by that point.
  - In 1988, McLaren-Honda with Senna and Prost dominated completely, winning 15 of 16 races (93.75% win rate). Senna took his first World Championship.
  - Fuel capacity limits and boost-pressure restrictions were phased in: 1987 boost capped at 4.0 bar; 1988 at 2.5 bar with 150-litre fuel limit; turbos banned entirely after 1988.
  - Senna's fatal crash at the 1994 San Marino Grand Prix (Tamburello curve) along with Roland Ratzenberger's death in qualifying that same weekend marked a watershed moment.

### 7. The 3.5-Litre High-Tech & Safety Revolution Era (1994-1999)
- **Characteristics:** Drastic regulatory interventions following tragedy; rapid elevation of crash safety; heavy introduction of computerised aids (then banned); naturally aspirated 3.5L V10s/V12s/V8s.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - The catastrophic 1994 San Marino Grand Prix at Imola saw the fatal crashes of Roland Ratzenberger and three-time champion Ayrton Senna.
  - The FIA under Max Mosley immediately implemented deep safety reforms: introduction of the underbody wooden skid block ("plank"), perforated airboxes, reduced engine displacement to 3.0L in 1995, raised cockpit sides, stringent crash-test standards.
  - Michael Schumacher won his first two titles in 1994 and 1995 with Benetton.
  - Damon Hill (1996) and Jacques Villeneuve (1997) took titles with Williams.
  - 1998 brought narrower cars (1.8m width) and grooved tyres (3 grooves front/4 rear, then 4 all around) to limit cornering speeds. The "narrow track" era from 1998 resulted in smaller rear tyres and a narrower track overall.
  - Mika Hakkinen and McLaren-Mercedes won back-to-back championships in 1998 and 1999.

### 8. The V10 Golden Era & Ferrari Hegemony (2000-2005)
- **Characteristics:** Screaming 3.0L naturally aspirated V10 engines revving over 19,000 RPM; massive manufacturer investments; Ferrari domination; electronic driver aids banned and briefly re-introduced.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Michael Schumacher and Scuderia Ferrari forged an unprecedented dynasty, winning five consecutive Drivers' Championships (2000-2004) and six consecutive Constructors' Championships (1999-2004).
  - 2002: Schumacher finished on the podium in every single race, clinching the title after just 11 rounds. 2004: He won 12 of the first 13 races.
  - Team orders were banned in 2002 after several incidents of manipulated race results, most famously the 2002 Austrian Grand Prix Ferrari team order.
  - 2005: The United States Grand Prix at Indianapolis saw seven out of ten teams refuse to race when their Michelin tyres were deemed unsafe-Bridgestone became the sole tyre supplier from 2007.
  - Fernando Alonso became F1's youngest champion at that time in 2005 (until Lewis Hamilton in 2008 and Sebastian Vettel in 2010), ending Ferrari's dominance.

### 9. The 2.4-Litre V8 Era & Red Bull Dominance (2006-2013)
- **Characteristics:** Engine downsizing to 2.4L V8s; engine freezes and rev limits; introduction of hybrid KERS and DRS; rise of privateer teams.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - Alonso won a second title in 2006. In 2007, rookie Lewis Hamilton and Alonso tied on points, allowing Ferrari's Kimi Raikkonen to snatch the championship by one point.
  - Hamilton won his first title dramatically in 2008 with a final-corner overtake in Brazil-the first of an eventual seven championships (equaling Schumacher's record).
  - 2008-2009: Honda, BMW, and Toyota all withdrew from F1 within a year, blaming the economic recession-ending manufacturer dominance. Honda's team went through a management buyout to become Brawn GP, which won both world championships in its sole season.
  - 2009: Major aero reset (wide low front wings, tall narrow rear wings, return to slick tyres). Brawn GP capitalized on the double diffuser.
  - Sebastian Vettel and Red Bull Racing, armed with Adrian Newey's blown-diffuser aerodynamics, dominated from 2010 to 2013, claiming four consecutive Drivers' and Constructors' crowns. Vettel became the youngest quadruple champion.
  - 2010: New teams accepted ahead of a "cost-cap": reborn Team Lotus (Malaysian consortium), Hispania Racing (first Spanish F1 team), Virgin Racing (Richard Branson), and US F1 Team. Most collapsed by 2014-2016.

### 10. The 1.6-Litre Turbo-Hybrid Era (2014-2021)
- **Characteristics:** Complex, whisper-quiet, ultra-efficient power units; Mercedes-AMG Petronas historic dominance; financial restructuring; sprint race format trials.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - 2014 brought 1.6L turbocharged V6 hybrid power units replacing the 2.4L naturally aspirated V8s. Dual MGU-K (kinetic energy recovery) and MGU-H (exhaust heat recovery). Strict 100 kg/hr fuel-flow limit.
  - Honda returned to the series in 2015 as the championship's fourth power-unit manufacturer.
  - Mercedes mastered the split-turbo architecture, embarking on eight consecutive Constructors' Championships (2014-2021). Lewis Hamilton won six titles in this era, surpassing 100 Grand Prix victories.
  - Nico Rosberg won the 2016 championship by just five points, then shockingly retired.
  - 2017-2018: Revised aerodynamics produced a title battle between Mercedes and Ferrari. Mercedes won both.
  - Max Verstappen became the youngest-ever race winner at age 18 in Spain (2016).
  - 2018: Introduction of the titanium Halo cockpit protection system.
  - 2021: One of the most intense championship fights in history between Hamilton and Verstappen, culminating in Verstappen winning his first title on the final lap in Abu Dhabi.
  - COVID-19 pandemic (2020-2021): Season start delayed; races held behind closed doors; 2021 car regulations pushed back to 2022; token system limiting which parts could be modified.
  - Sprint qualifying format trialed in 2021 at three race weekends. From 2023, sprint races no longer impact main race grid position; separate sprint shootout introduced.

### 11. Modern Ground-Effect Era & Cost-Cap Regulation (2022-Present)
- **Characteristics:** Aerodynamic reset emphasizing 3D contoured underfloor ground-effect tunnels, 18-inch low-profile tyres, sustainable E10 fuel, financial cost parity, new entrants.
- **Key Highlights:**
  - 2022: Radical aerodynamic reboot-return of full-length contoured underfloor venturi tunnels (ground effect), simplified front wing, disappearance of complex bargeboards, over-wheel winglets, and switch to 18-inch low-profile Pirelli tyres. Goals: reduce dirty air, promote closer racing and overtaking.
  - Red Bull Racing and Max Verstappen dominated: 2022-Verstappen won 15 races. 2023-Verstappen won 19 of 22 races (86.4% win rate); Red Bull won 21 of 22 races (95.5%), breaking McLaren's 1988 record. 2024-Verstappen claimed his fourth consecutive title.
  - McLaren surged back to win their first Constructors' Championship since 1998 in 2024, following their desperate 2023 sit at the bottom of the grid.
  - In August 2020, all ten F1 teams signed a new Concorde Agreement committing them to the sport until 2025, including a $145 million budget cap.
  - 2026: Cost cap restructured to $215 million operational base limit with a separate $130 million Power Unit Cost Cap. Audi becomes a works team (Sauber rebranded). Cadillac received approval as an 11th team for 2026. Engine hybrid ratio shifting from 53/47 to 58/42 (2027) and 60/40 (2028).
  - 2025: Lando Norris (McLaren) captures his maiden World Drivers' Championship, concluding the ground-effect regulation cycle.

## 3. Key Facts: Major Rule Changes

### Powertrain & Engine Regulations
| Period | Regulation |
|---|---|
| 1947-1953 | 4.5L naturally aspirated or 1.5L supercharged. (1952-1953 ran to 2.0L F2 specs) |
| 1954-1960 | Downsized to 2.5L naturally aspirated or 750cc supercharged. Alcohol/methanol fuel banned in 1958. |
| 1961-1965 | Downsized to 1.5L naturally aspirated (no forced induction). |
| 1966-1985 | "Return to Power": 3.0L naturally aspirated or 1.5L forced induction. |
| 1986-1988 | Phased turbo restrictions: 1987 boost capped at 4.0 bar; 1988 at 2.5 bar + 150L fuel limit. |
| 1989-1994 | Turbos banned; 3.5L naturally aspirated engines (up to 12 cylinders). |
| 1995-2005 | Displacement reduced to 3.0L NA. V10 cylinder architecture made mandatory in 2000. |
| 2006-2013 | 2.4L naturally aspirated 90-degree V8. Rev limits: 19,000 RPM (2007), 18,000 RPM (2009). Engine frozen. |
| 2014-Present | 1.6L 90-degree turbo V6 hybrid with direct injection, max 15,000 RPM, 100 kg/hr fuel-flow, dual ERS (MGU-K + MGU-H). |
| 2022 Fuel | Shift to E10 fuel (10% sustainable bio-ethanol blend). |
| 2026+ | Operational cost cap raised to $215M base + $130M PU cost cap. Engine-to-hybrid ratio shifting to 58/42 (2027) and 60/40 (2028). |

### Aerodynamics & Chassis Regulations
| Year | Change |
|---|---|
| 1962 | Stress-bearing monocoque chassis introduced (Lotus 25), replacing tubular spaceframes. |
| 1968 | Aerodynamic wings first appeared (Lotus 49B at Monaco). Sponsorship liveries introduced. |
| 1969 | Ban on high, movable wings mounted to suspension uprights; wings fixed directly to chassis. |
| 1977-1982 | Ground-effect underbody tunnels with sliding skirts. Skirts banned 1981; venturi tunnels banned for 1983. |
| 1981 | McLaren MP4/1 introduces carbon-fibre composite monocoque chassis. |
| 1994 | Stepped floor and central 10 mm wooden/composite skid block ("plank") introduced to limit ground clearance. |
| 1998 | Car width narrowed from 2,000 mm to 1,800 mm. Grooved tyres introduced (3 grooves front/4 rear). |
| 2009 | Aero simplification: removal of winglets/bargeboards; wider, lower front wings; tall narrow rear wings; slick tyres reintroduced. |
| 2011 | Drag Reduction System (DRS) introduced-driver-activated rear wing flap in designated zones. |
| 2014 | Hybrid turbocharged V6 engines introduced. |
| 2017 | Wider chassis (2,000 mm), wider delta-shaped wings, wider tyres (305 mm front / 405 mm rear). |
| 2018 | Mandatory titanium Halo cockpit protection. |
| 2022 | Radical aero reboot: ground-effect underfloor venturi tunnels, simplified front wing, 18-inch low-profile tyres, ban on bargeboards. |

### Driver Aids & Electronics
| Year | Change |
|---|---|
| 1983 | Active suspension first appeared (Lotus 92). Perfected by 1987; Senna won Monaco in 1987 with it. |
| 1994 | Electronic driver aids (active suspension, traction control, launch control, ABS) banned. |
| 2001 | Traction control and launch control briefly re-legalized due to enforcement difficulties. |
| 2004 | Launch control and full-automatic gearboxes re-banned. |
| 2008 | Traction control permanently banned; standard FIA ECUs introduced. |
| 2011 | Drag Reduction System (DRS) introduced for designated overtaking zones. |

### Safety Milestones & Equipment
| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1968 | Dan Gurney introduces the full-face helmet to F1. |
| 1972 | Mandatory safety foam in fuel tanks; 6-point harnesses. |
| 1981 | Mandatory survival cell extending to driver's feet; crash-testing introduced. |
| 1994-1996 | Extensive cockpit restructuring after Imola: raised cockpit sides, headrest padding, stringent dynamic impact tests. |
| 2003 | Mandatory HANS (Head and Neck Support) device. |
| 2014 | FIA accident panel established after Jules Bianchi's crash; leads to Virtual Safety Car (VSC) system. |
| 2018 | Mandatory titanium Halo cockpit protection-proven lifesaving (Grosjean Bahrain 2020, Zhou Silverstone 2022). |

### Sporting & Financial Rules
| Year | Change |
|---|---|
| 1958 | Constructors' Championship created. |
| 1978 | Bernie Ecclestone becomes FOCA president; begins commercial restructuring. |
| 1981 | First Concorde Agreement signed-codifies TV revenue sharing and governance. |
| 1984 | Mid-race refuelling banned. |
| 1994 | Mid-race refuelling reintroduced. |
| 1992 & 1997 | Second and third Concorde Agreements signed. |
| 2002 | Team orders banned after manipulated race results (notably 2002 Austrian GP). |
| 2005 | Michelin tyre crisis at US Grand Prix; 7/10 teams refused to race. |
| 2010 | Points system overhauled to modern 25-18-15-12-10-8-6-4-2-1 for top 10. Mid-race refuelling permanently banned. |
| 2019 | 1 bonus point for fastest lap (drivers finishing top 10). |
| 2020 | All 10 teams sign new Concorde Agreement until 2025 with $145M budget cap. |
| 2021 | Cost Cap introduced at $145M (reduced to $140M in 2022, $135M baseline thereafter). |
| 2023 | Standardized sprint weekend format established. |
| 2026 | Cost cap restructured to $215M operational + $130M PU cost cap. 22-car grid. |

## 6. Additional Reference Notes

### Key Figures
- **Giuseppe "Nino" Farina** - First World Champion (1950)
- **Juan Manuel Fangio** - 5 titles; record 46 years; highest winning percentage
- **Michael Schumacher** - 7 titles; 5 consecutive (2000-2004); record equaled by Hamilton and Verstappen
- **Lewis Hamilton** - 7 titles; 100+ race wins; most pole positions; most points
- **Max Verstappen** - Youngest race winner (18); 4 consecutive titles (2021-2024); record 19 wins in a season (2023)
- **Ayrton Senna** - 3 titles; 1988 Monaco qualifying lap record; 1988-1990 Senna-Prost rivalry
- **Niki Lauda** - 3 titles; 0.5-point championship margin (1984); miraculous comeback after Nurburgring crash
- **Alain Prost** - 4 titles; "The Professor"
- **Jim Clark** - 2 titles; dominated 1960s; also won Indy 500
- **Jack Brabham** - 3 titles; only driver to win championship in own car
- **John Surtees** - Only person to win world championships on two and four wheels
- **Jochen Rindt** - Only posthumous World Champion (1970)

### Tyres
- **1998-2008:** Grooved tyres (3-4 grooves) to reduce cornering speeds
- **2009-Present:** Slick tyres reintroduced; currently exclusively supplied by Pirelli
- Three dry compounds must be used in each race; minimum one pit stop required

### Circuits
- **British and Italian Grands Prix** are the only events held every F1 season
- **Monaco Grand Prix** first held in 1929, run continuously since 1955 (except 2020)
- **Singapore 2008** - first F1 night race; followed by Abu Dhabi 2009 and Bahrain 2014
- **1950:** 7 races, 6 in Europe + Indianapolis 500 (different regulations)
- **2024:** 24 races-highest number of World Championship races in one season

### Commercial Structure
- **Concorde Agreements:** 1981 (first), 1992 (second), 1997 (third), 2020 (fourth-until 2025)
- **Entry fee:** $450 million up-front payment for new teams, shared among existing teams
- **Driver Super Licence:** Highest FIA racing licence

### Physical Demands
- Drivers burn ~1,000 calories per hour; lose up to 5% body weight per race
- Cornering forces up to 6.5g; braking 6g; acceleration 2g
