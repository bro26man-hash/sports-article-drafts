## 1. Overview

Formula One (F1) is the highest class of worldwide racing for open-wheel, single-seater formula racing cars, run by the Formula One Group and sanctioned by the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA). The FIA Formula One World Championship has been one of the world's premier forms of motorsport since its inaugural running in **1950** and is widely considered the **pinnacle of motorsport**.

The word "formula" in the name refers to the set of rules all participant cars must follow. A Formula One season consists of a series of races known as **Grands Prix**, held in multiple countries on purpose-built circuits or closed public roads (street circuits).

Points are awarded to determine two annual World Championships: one for **drivers** and one for **constructors** (teams). Each driver must hold a valid **Super Licence** — the highest class of racing licence the FIA issues — and races must be held on **Grade One** tracks, the highest circuit rating.

Commercial rights to F1 are leased to **Liberty Media Corporation**, which acquired them in **2017** for an estimated **$8 billion**, previously owned by British business magnate **Bernie Ecclestone**. Liberty added five Grands Prix to the calendar: Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Dutch.

Formula One cars are the world's fastest regulated road-course racing cars, owing to high cornering speeds achieved by generating large amounts of aerodynamic downforce — most of which is generated by front and rear wings as well as underbody tunnels. The cars depend on electronics, aerodynamics, suspension, and tyres.

## 2. History & Evolution — Focused Summary

### Origins (1946–1950)
- Formula One originated from two predecessor championships: the **World Manufacturers' Championship (1925–1927)** and the **European Drivers' Championship (1931–1939)**.
- Before WWII, several Grand Prix organisations proposed a new championship, but the war prevented formalisation.
- The "International Formula" was agreed upon in **1946**, effective in **1947**.
- The first Grand Prix under the new regulations was the **1946 Turin Grand Prix**.
- The **World Championship** was officially instituted to commence in **1950**.
- The first World Championship race was the **1950 British Grand Prix** at **Silverstone** on **13 May 1950**.

### The Fangio Era (1950s)
- **Giuseppe Farina** won the first Drivers' World Championship in 1950, narrowly defeating his teammate **Juan Manuel Fangio**.
- Fangio went on to win the championship in **1951, 1954, 1955, 1956, and 1957** — five titles that set the record for the most World Championships won by a single driver, a record that stood for **46 years** until **Michael Schumacher** won his sixth in 2003.
- Fangio won **24 of the 52 races** he entered — still the record for the **highest winning percentage** by an individual driver (46%).
- **Stirling Moss** is often regarded as one of the greatest drivers never to have won the championship; he finished 2nd four times and 3rd three times between 1955 and 1961.
- Non-championship F1 races continued until **1983**, when the last one — the **Race of Champions at Brands Hatch** — was held.

### The Constructors' Championship & Technological Revolution (1958–1960s)
- A **Constructors' Championship** was added for the **1958 season**.
- **1959**: **Jack Brabham** (world champion 1959, 1960, 1966) proved the **mid-engined car's** superiority; by **1961**, all teams had switched to mid-engine layout. The **Ferguson P99**, a four-wheel drive design, was the last front-engined F1 car to enter a championship race (1961 British GP).
- **1962**: **Lotus** introduced the **aluminium-sheet monocoque chassis**, replacing the traditional space-frame design — called "the greatest technological breakthrough since the introduction of mid-engined cars."
- **1968**: **Sponsorship was introduced**. **Team Gunston** was first (cigarette branding on Brabham cars at the 1968 South African GP). **Lotus** followed at the **1968 Spanish GP** with Gold Leaf livery (Imperial Tobacco).
- **1968**: **Aerodynamic downforce** entered the sport. **Colin Chapman** of Lotus installed modest front wings and a rear spoiler on the **Lotus 49B** at the **1968 Monaco Grand Prix**.

### Ground Effect & the Big-Business Era (1970s–1980s)
- **Late 1970s**: **Lotus introduced ground-effect aerodynamics** (previously used on Jim Hall's **Chaparral 2J** in 1970), generating enormous downforce and dramatically increasing cornering speeds. Aerodynamic forces could reach **up to 5× the car's weight**. Extremely stiff springs were needed to maintain constant ride height, leaving suspension virtually solid — drivers depended entirely on tyres for cushioning.
- **1970s**: **Bernie Ecclestone** transformed F1's commercial landscape. He bought **Brabham** in 1971, gained a seat on **FOCA** (Formula One Constructors' Association), and became its president in **1978**. He restructured revenue flow — persuading teams to "hunt as a pack" and offering circuits a package deal in return for trackside advertising.
- **1977**: **Renault pioneered turbocharged engines** in F1.
- **1979**: Formation of **FISA** (Fédération Internationale du Sport Automobile) triggered the **FISA–FOCA war** — a bitter struggle over TV revenues and technical regulations.
- **1981**: The **Concorde Agreement** was signed, guaranteeing technical stability by giving teams reasonable notice of new regulations. Teams signed subsequent agreements in **1992** and **1997**.
- **1983**: **FISA banned ground-effect aerodynamics**.
- **1984**: FIA limited **fuel tank capacity** to reduce engine power.
- **1986**: A **BMW turbocharged engine** achieved an estimated **over 1,300 bhp** (970 kW) in qualifying at the Italian Grand Prix — the most powerful open-wheel circuit cars ever. By 1987, race trim power reached around **1,100 bhp** with boost limited to 4 bar.
- **1988**: FIA limited **boost pressures**.
- **1989**: **Turbocharged engines were banned completely**.

### Electronic Aids, the Senna–Prost Rivalry & Tragedy (1980s–1990s)
- **1983**: **Lotus** introduced **active suspension** on the **Lotus 92**.
- **1987**: **Ayrton Senna** won Monaco with active suspension perfected.
- **Early 1990s**: **Semi-automatic gearboxes** and **traction control** became common.
- **1988–1993**: The **Senna–Prost rivalry** dominated the sport, becoming F1's central focus.
- **1994 San Marino Grand Prix**: **Ayrton Senna died** after crashing into a wall at the **Tamburello curve**. **Roland Ratzenberger** also died in a qualifying accident that same weekend.
- **1994**: The FIA banned **traction control, launch control, automatic shifting**, and other electronic driver aids — citing complaints that technology was determining outcomes more than driver skill. The ban made cars very "twitchy" and difficult to drive.

### The Narrow-Track Era & Safety Awakening (1998–2000s)
- **1998–2008**: The **"narrow-track era"** — smaller rear tyres, narrower track overall, and **grooved tyres** to reduce mechanical grip and cornering speeds. The FIA's stated objective was to reduce cornering speeds and produce racing similar to rainy conditions. This era followed Senna's death and growing safety concerns.
- **Senna's and Ratzenberger's deaths** marked a turning point: the FIA began using safety as a reason to impose rule changes without needing full team agreement under the Concorde Agreement.
- **2001**: Electronic driver aids were **briefly reintroduced**.
- **2002**: **Team orders were banned** after several incidents of manipulated race results (most famously **Ferrari** at the **2002 Austrian Grand Prix**).
- **2004 & 2008**: Electronic aids **banned again**.
- **2005 US Grand Prix**: **Seven of ten teams withdrew** when **Michelin tyres** were deemed unsafe for use at Indianapolis. This led to **Bridgestone** becoming the sole tyre supplier from **2007** by default, with a three-year contract signed in December 2007.

### Schumacher's Dominance & the Manufacturers' Return (2000s)
- **Michael Schumacher and Ferrari** won **five consecutive Drivers' Championships** and **six consecutive Constructors' Championships**.
- Schumacher set records for Grand Prix wins, wins in a season, and most Drivers' Championships.
- **2005**: **Fernando Alonso** became F1's youngest champion at that time (later broken by **Lewis Hamilton** in 2008 and **Sebastian Vettel** in 2010).
- **2006**: Renault and Alonso won both titles again. Schumacher retired after 16 years in F1.
- **2008 & 2009**: **Honda, BMW, and Toyota** all withdrew within a year, blaming the **economic recession** — ending manufacturer dominance.
- **2009**: **Brawn GP** (born from Honda's management buyout, led by Ross Brawn and Nick Fry) won both championships in its only season — one of sport's greatest fairy tales.
- **Mercedes-Benz re-entered** as a manufacturer in **2010** after purchasing Brawn GP.

### The Hybrid Era (2014–Present)
- **2014**: Major rule change — **2.4L naturally aspirated V8s replaced by 1.6L turbocharged hybrid power units**. This prompted **Honda to return** as a power-unit manufacturer in **2015**.
- **Mercedes emerged as dominant**, winning **16 of 19 races** in 2014; continued dominance through 2016 (**19 wins in 2016 — a record**). **Lewis Hamilton** won titles in 2014, 2015; **Nico Rosberg** won in 2016 (by just **5 points**), then retired.
- **2016**: **Max Verstappen** became the **youngest-ever race winner** at age **18** in Spain. **Haas** joined the grid.
- **2017–2018**: Revised aerodynamic regulations produced title battles between **Mercedes and Ferrari**. Mercedes won both.
- **2020**: All 10 teams signed a new **Concorde Agreement** committing to the sport until **2025**, including a **$145 million budget cap** for car development.
- **COVID-19 pandemic**: The significant 2021 technical overhaul was pushed to **2022**; constructors used 2020 chassis for two seasons; a **token system** limited which parts could be modified. Seasons were subject to postponements, cancellations, and behind-closed-doors races.
- **2021**: **Max Verstappen** beat **Lewis Hamilton** to the Drivers' Championship after a season-long battle that saw the pair exchange the championship lead multiple times. The **first sprint races** were trialed at three race weekends.
- **2022**: Major rule and car design change — **reintroduction of ground effects**, new aerodynamics, **larger 18-inch wheels with low-profile tyres**, redesigned nose and wing regulations, **barge boards banned**. Goal: promote **closer racing** by reducing "dirty air." **Red Bull and Verstappen** dominated; won 2022 and 2023 championships.
- **2024**: Significant sponsorship reshuffling — **Sauber** became **Stake F1 Team Kick Sauber**; **AlphaTauri** rebranded as **Visa Cash App RB (VCARB)**. F1 announced partnerships with **Mattel (Hot Wheels)** and **Lego**.
- **2026**: **Audi** enters as a works team (Sauber rebrands); **Cadillac** approved as **11th team**.
- **2026**: Cost cap restructured to **$215 million** base operational limit (absorbing previously excluded expenditures); new **$130 million Power Unit Cost Cap** implemented to regulate power unit manufacturers.
- **2027/28**: Engine-to-hybrid power split shifts from **53/47 → 58/42 → 60/40**; max internal combustion output up to **450 kW**; feedback from drivers about energy management challenges driving these changes.

## 7. Business & Commercial Evolution

### The Ecclestone Era
- Bernie Ecclestone bought Brabham in **1971**, gaining FOCA membership.
- Became FOCA president in **1978**.
- Transformed F1 from a collection of independent races into a **multi-billion dollar business**.
- Pioneered the **package deal** for circuits (TV package + trackside advertising).
- Negotiated three Concorde Agreements (1981, 1992, 1997).

### The Liberty Media Era
- **Liberty Media** acquired commercial rights in **2017** for an estimated **$8 billion**.
- Added five new Grands Prix: **Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Dutch**.
- Introduced **F1 TV** streaming platform (2018).
- New **logo** unveiled in 2017 (replaced the "flying one" from 1993).
- **Concorde Agreement** signed in 2020, committing all teams through 2025.
- **$145M budget cap** introduced (2022) for car development.
- **$215M operational cost cap** (2026) with separate **$130M Power Unit Cost Cap**.

## 9. Current Landscape & Future Directions

### 2026 Grid & Calendar
- **11 teams** including new entrants **Cadillac** and **Audi** (works entry via Sauber)
- **24 races** planned for 2026
- New venues: Miami, Las Vegas, Saudi Arabia, Qatar
- **Sauber** → Stake F1 Team Kick Sauber (until 2025) → **Audi works team** (2026+)
- **AlphaTauri** → **Visa Cash App RB (VCARB)**

### Environmental Initiatives
- F1目标: **carbon neutral by 2030**
- **E10 fuel** (10% ethanol) since 2021; 100% sustainable fuel planned for 2025/2026
- All events to be "sustainable" by 2025 (eliminating single-use plastics, waste recycling/composting)
- 2019 emissions: **256,000 tonnes CO2** (45% from logistics; only 0.7% from cars)
- FIA signed **UN Sports for Climate Action** framework (2020)

### Social Initiatives
- **#WeRaceAsOne** initiative (2020): anti-racism solidarity; diversity and opportunity task force
- Mercedes' **Accelerate 25**: 25% new hires from underrepresented minorities by 2025
- **F1 Academy** (2022): female single-seater championship; inaugural winner: Marta García
- Female viewership grew from **20% (2019)** to **40% (2022)** — partly attributed to *Drive to Survive*
